Integrating, Testing, and Maintaining a Complete Robotic System

Summary

The capstone chapter of the course, where every earlier skill comes together. Students learn to select components (sensors, actuators) and hardware platforms for a robotic design, then assemble, wire, and integrate a complete system. The chapter covers systematic testing, reverse-engineering and documentation practices, and finishes with robot performance evaluation, maintenance, and a look at current trends shaping the robotics industry.

The Capstone: From Kit to Original Design

!!! abstract "Why This Chapter Is Different" Every chapter before this one taught you one piece of the puzzle: building with a kit, writing code, designing custom parts, wiring circuits, choosing sensors, understanding mechanisms, controlling feedback loops, and programming advanced behaviors. This chapter does not teach a new piece. It teaches you how all the pieces fit together—the process professional engineers and competitive robotics teams use to go from a blank sheet of paper to a complete, tested, working robot.

Throughout this chapter, follow a running example: a fictional FTC (FIRST Tech Challenge) team called Vertex Robotics, preparing for a new competition season called "Vertex Rising." In this game, robots must locate scattered rings on the field, pick them up, and stack them onto scoring poles at three different heights within a two-and-a-half-minute match. Vertex Robotics has outgrown the StarterBot-style kit robot from Chapter 1. This season, they are designing an original robot from scratch, and every decision they make will draw on a chapter you have already studied.

Robotic components are the categories of physical and electronic parts that combine to form any robot, regardless of its specific purpose. Before selecting a single part, a design team should inventory the categories of components their robot will need, because forgetting a category during planning is far more expensive to fix after assembly has begun.

Every robot, from a simple line-following kit robot to a competition-grade FTC machine, is built from the same broad categories of components:

  • Structural components — the chassis, frame, brackets, and fasteners that hold everything in place (covered in this chapter and Chapter 5)
  • Power components — batteries, voltage regulators, and distribution wiring that supply energy to every other subsystem (Chapter 6)
  • Actuation components — motors, servos, and pneumatic or hydraulic cylinders that create motion (Chapters 9 and 10)
  • Sensing components — sensors that gather information about the robot and its environment (Chapter 8)
  • Control and computation components — the microcontroller or computer that runs the robot's program and makes decisions (Chapter 12)
  • Software — the code that ties sensing, decision-making, and actuation together (Chapters 3, 4, and 13)

Vertex Robotics' first planning meeting is essentially this list, turned into a checklist. Before choosing any specific part, the team asks: what does each category need to accomplish for the Vertex Rising game task of finding, collecting, and stacking rings?

Choosing a Frame: Robot Chassis and Robotic Hardware Platform

A robot chassis is the structural frame that physically supports and connects a robot's other components, much like a car's frame supports its engine, seats, and body panels. The chassis determines the robot's overall size, weight distribution, ground clearance, and how easily new parts can be added or removed later.

Design teams rarely start a chassis from nothing. A robotic hardware platform is a baseline combination of chassis, drivetrain, and core electronics that a team either buys as a starting kit or designs from scratch to serve as the foundation for a specific robot. Choosing a platform is one of the first and most consequential decisions in the entire design process, because it constrains almost every choice that follows.

Vertex Robotics has to decide between three broad platform strategies, each with real tradeoffs:

Platform Strategy Description Advantage Tradeoff
Kit-based platform Pre-manufactured chassis rails and drivetrain (e.g., a goBILDA or REV starter kit) Fast to assemble, proven reliability Less unique, may not fit unusual mechanisms
Hybrid platform Kit drivetrain with custom-designed superstructure (from Chapter 5's fabrication skills) Balances speed and originality Requires integrating two design sources cleanly
Fully custom platform Every structural part designed and fabricated by the team Maximum design freedom Highest time cost and risk of structural failure

Vertex Robotics chooses the hybrid platform: a proven kit drivetrain for reliable driving, combined with a custom 3D-printed and laser-cut superstructure—designed using the same CAD and fabrication skills from Chapter 5—built specifically to collect and stack rings.

Chassis material choice matters just as much as platform strategy. Common options include aluminum extrusion (strong, reusable, moderately heavy), polycarbonate sheet (lightweight, easy to cut, less rigid over long spans), 3D-printed plastic (highly customizable, limited by printer size and strength), and carbon fiber (very strong and light, but expensive and harder to modify). Most student teams mix materials: aluminum extrusion for the main frame, with 3D-printed or laser-cut brackets for custom mounting points.

Powering the Robot: The Power System

A robot power system is the complete set of components that stores, regulates, and distributes electrical energy to every powered part of the robot. Get the power system wrong, and nothing else in this chapter matters—a robot with a brilliant sensor layout and perfectly wired actuators is useless if its battery cannot deliver enough current or its wiring melts under load.

Drawing on the electrical fundamentals from Chapter 6 and the circuit design skills from Chapter 7, a complete power system typically includes:

  1. A main battery, sized to supply enough voltage and current for the robot's motors and electronics for a full match or work session
  2. A main breaker or fuse, a safety device that interrupts current flow if a short circuit or overload occurs, protecting both the robot and the people near it
  3. Voltage regulator modules, which step battery voltage down (or occasionally up) to the specific voltages different components require—a drive motor and a microcontroller rarely run on the same voltage
  4. A power distribution harness, the wiring that carries power from the battery, through the breaker and regulators, out to every actuator and sensor
  5. A battery indicator or monitoring circuit, which alerts the team when voltage drops too low to operate safely

!!! warning A power system is only as strong as its weakest connection. A loose battery connector or an undersized wire gauge can cause voltage to sag exactly when a robot needs maximum power—for example, when lifting a stack of rings. Always match wire gauge and connector type to the actual current the circuit will carry.

Selecting Actuators and Sensors for the Job

With a chassis and power system chosen, Vertex Robotics faces the question every design team faces next: which specific actuators and sensors actually accomplish the game task? Robot actuator selection is the process of matching a required motion—lifting, gripping, rotating, driving—to the actuator type, size, and specification best suited to produce it. Robot sensor selection is the parallel process of matching a required piece of information—distance, color, orientation, contact—to the sensor type best suited to measure it.

Neither selection happens in isolation. Actuator selection depends on the mechanical advantage available from gears, levers, and linkages (Chapter 9), and on whether the motion needs the smooth continuous force of an electric motor or the fast linear push of a pneumatic cylinder (Chapter 10). Sensor selection depends on the coordinate systems and measurement concepts from Chapter 8, and every selected sensor and actuator ultimately reports to or is commanded by the control system and code covered in Chapters 11 through 13.

For the Vertex Rising task, the team breaks their robot's job into sub-tasks and, for each one, asks what physical requirement must be met and which chapter's concepts help answer it:

  • Detecting a ring's location on the field — requires a sensor that reports distance and possibly color, drawing on Chapter 8's sensor tour
  • Driving toward and collecting a ring — requires drivetrain motors sized for torque and speed, drawing on Chapter 9's mechanical advantage concepts
  • Lifting rings to three different scoring heights — requires an actuator capable of controlled, repeatable vertical motion, potentially a motor with a lead screw or a pneumatic cylinder from Chapter 10
  • Confirming a ring is securely gripped before lifting — requires a contact or presence sensor, preventing wasted lift cycles

Putting It Together: Robot Assembly and Robot Wiring

With components selected, the physical build begins. Robot assembly is the process of physically mounting, fastening, and connecting all selected components onto the chassis according to the design plan. Good assembly practice follows a deliberate order, not a random one:

  1. Mount the heaviest components (battery, main drive motors) low and near the chassis's center to keep the robot's center of gravity stable
  2. Install structural subassemblies (arms, lifts, grippers) before running any wiring through them
  3. Leave planned access points for batteries, fuses, and connectors that will need frequent attention during competition
  4. Fasten everything securely before wiring, since a part that shifts after wiring is run can stress or disconnect a wire

Once the physical structure is assembled, robot wiring is the task of routing and connecting electrical conductors between the power system, the microcontroller, and every actuator and sensor, following a planned wiring diagram rather than improvising connections in the moment. Poor wiring is one of the most common causes of robot failure in competition, precisely because problems it causes—an intermittent connection, a pinched wire—can be difficult to diagnose once covers are back on.

System Integration: Making the Subsystems Act as One

System integration is the process of combining a robot's chassis, power system, actuators, sensors, and control software so that they operate together as a single coherent machine rather than as a collection of independently functioning parts. Integration is where a robot's individual subsystems either prove they can cooperate—or reveal conflicts that assembly and wiring alone could not expose.

Integration problems are often subtle. A sensor might work perfectly on a test bench but report unreliable readings once mounted near a motor that generates electrical noise. A lift mechanism might move correctly by itself but stall when the drivetrain draws current at the same moment, starving the lift motor of voltage. Recognizing these interactions is exactly why system integration is treated as its own concept, distinct from assembly and wiring.

Robot Testing: Proving It Works Before It Counts

Robot testing is the systematic process of verifying that a robot's subsystems—and the robot as a whole—function correctly, performed in stages of increasing complexity rather than all at once. Testing everything simultaneously on a fully assembled robot makes it nearly impossible to isolate the source of a problem, so experienced teams test in a deliberate sequence:

  1. Power-on test — verify the power system delivers correct voltage to every subsystem before anything else is checked
  2. Subsystem test — verify each actuator and sensor works correctly in isolation, disconnected from the rest of the robot's behavior
  3. Integration test — verify subsystems work correctly together, running actual control code that combines sensor input and actuator output
  4. Field test — verify the fully integrated robot performs its intended task under realistic, competition-like conditions

Each stage tends to expose a different category of problem, and catching an issue early—during a power-on test rather than a field test—saves enormous time. When Testing Reveals Problems: Reengineering and Documentation

Testing rarely goes perfectly on the first attempt, and that is expected, not a failure of the design process. Robot reengineering is the practice of revising a robot's design—its components, wiring, or software—in direct response to problems discovered during testing, rather than treating the original design as final. When Vertex Robotics' integration test reveals that their lift motor stalls whenever the drivetrain accelerates, reengineering might mean adding a dedicated power regulator for the lift, upgrading wire gauge, or reconsidering the gear ratio from Chapter 9 to reduce the current the lift motor demands.

Reengineering only works well when a team can remember what they tried before and why. Robot documentation is the ongoing written and visual record of a robot's design decisions, wiring diagrams, code versions, and test results, kept throughout the design process rather than reconstructed from memory afterward. Good documentation turns "I think we tried that already" into a quick, confident answer.

Strong robot documentation typically includes several distinct artifacts, each serving a different purpose:

Documentation Artifact Purpose
Engineering notebook Chronological record of design decisions, sketches, and reasoning (often required for FTC judging)
Wiring diagram Visual reference showing every electrical connection, prevents guesswork during repairs
CAD files Digital models of custom parts, enabling reprinting or redesign (from Chapter 5)
Code repository Version history of all programs, allowing the team to revert a change that broke something
Test logs Dated records of what was tested, what failed, and what fix was applied

!!! tip In FTC competitions, judges often weight the engineering notebook as heavily as the robot's on-field performance. A well-documented reengineering decision—showing the problem, the reasoning, and the result—demonstrates the engineering process itself, which is exactly what the notebook is meant to capture.

Judging the Result: Performance Evaluation and Maintenance

Once a robot is built, tested, and refined, a team needs an honest answer to a deceptively simple question: how good is it? Robot performance evaluation is the process of measuring a robot's behavior against specific, ideally quantitative, criteria—rather than relying on a general impression of "it seems to work."

For Vertex Robotics, meaningful performance criteria for the ring-stacking task might include:

  • Cycle time — average seconds to collect one ring and stack it, measured across multiple attempts
  • Accuracy — percentage of scoring attempts that successfully land a ring on the intended pole
  • Reliability — number of matches completed without an unplanned mechanical or electrical failure
  • Consistency — how much cycle time and accuracy vary from attempt to attempt, not just their average

Performance evaluation only has value if it happens repeatedly, because a robot's performance changes over time even without redesign—parts wear, connectors loosen, and batteries age. Robot maintenance is the ongoing practice of scheduled and preventive upkeep tasks that keep a robot performing reliably over its working life, rather than waiting for a failure to force a repair.

A practical robot maintenance checklist, drawn from the power, wiring, and mechanical concepts covered throughout this course, includes:

  1. Fully charge and inspect batteries before each use, checking for swelling or damage
  2. Check all connectors and fasteners for looseness after transport or heavy use
  3. Inspect drivetrain components (from Chapter 9) for wear, especially gears, belts, and wheels
  4. Re-verify sensor calibration periodically, since drift accumulates over time (Chapter 8)
  5. Back up code and documentation after every significant change
  6. Replace consumable or high-wear parts proactively, before they fail during a match

Looking Ahead: Robotics Industry Trends

This course began with a kit robot and formal vocabulary in Chapter 2, including a first look at robotics careers. It is fitting to close by looking outward again—at where the field of robotics is actually heading, and how the skills built across this entire course connect to it. Robotics industry trends are the current directions of change and growth shaping the robotics field, reflecting new technologies, new applications, and new demands from industries adopting robots.

Several trends are reshaping robotics right now, and each one builds directly on concepts from this course:

  • AI-powered perception — robots increasingly use machine learning to interpret sensor data (building on Chapter 8's sensors and Chapter 13's advanced programming), enabling them to recognize objects rather than just measure simple quantities
  • Collaborative robots (cobots) — robots designed to work safely alongside humans in shared spaces, requiring careful control system design (Chapter 11) to guarantee safe, predictable behavior
  • Autonomous mobile robots — robots that navigate and make decisions without constant human control, combining every layer of this course from sensing to control to software
  • Soft and flexible robotics — actuators and structures made from compliant materials rather than rigid parts, expanding the actuator selection choices covered earlier in this chapter
  • Swarm and multi-robot systems — many simple robots coordinating to accomplish a task no single robot could do alone

You Are Ready to Design

None of that was new theory—it was the deliberate combination of everything you built and learned in Chapters 1 through 13, applied to a single, original machine of your own design.

That is the actual meaning of "capstone." You did not just learn about robotics one topic at a time. You now hold a complete, repeatable process for taking a robot from an idea to a competition-ready machine, the same process used by professional robotics engineers and FTC teams around the world. Whatever comes next—a new FTC season, a FIRST Robotics Competition build, a personal project, or a first step toward a robotics career—you are ready to design it yourself.
