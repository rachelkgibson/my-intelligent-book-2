Electronic Components and Circuit Design

Summary

This chapter moves from electrical theory to electronic practice. Students learn to identify and use core components—resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, and LEDs—and build hands-on skills including breadboarding, soldering, and multimeter use. The chapter also covers circuit diagrams, printed circuit boards, batteries and power supplies, switches, relays, and motor drivers, culminating in circuit design and simulation.

From Theory to Practice

Chapter 6 gave you the vocabulary of electricity: voltage, current, resistance, Ohm's Law, series and parallel circuits, and the safety habits that keep a lab bench safe. That vocabulary was necessary, but abstract. You calculated current with a formula; you did not yet hold the part current flows through in your hand. This chapter closes that gap, teaching you to identify the physical components in every StarterBot circuit, read the diagrams describing how they connect, and practice hands-on skills—breadboarding, soldering, and multimeter use—that turn a diagram into a working circuit.

By the end of the chapter, you will be able to look at a real circuit board, name most of what is on it, and explain why each part is there. You will also design and simulate a simple circuit of your own before ever touching a soldering iron, which is exactly how professional engineers work: think first, build second.

Circuit Diagram: The Blueprint of a Circuit

Before you can build a circuit, you need a way to describe it that does not depend on drawing every wire exactly as it will be routed in real life. A circuit diagram (also called a schematic) is a standardized drawing that represents electronic components as symbols and shows how they connect electrically, without depicting their physical size, shape, or position. Just as an architect's blueprint uses standard symbols for doors and windows regardless of what brand is installed, a circuit diagram uses standard symbols for resistors, batteries, and switches regardless of what the physical part looks like.

Reading a circuit diagram is a skill, not an instinct, and it rewards a specific strategy:

  1. Find the power source first (battery or power supply symbol) and identify its two terminals.
  2. Trace the wire from the positive terminal, component by component, following the loop.
  3. Note every component the current passes through and in what order.
  4. Confirm the loop returns to the negative terminal, completing the circuit.

A schematic only becomes useful once you can connect each symbol to a real part sitting on your workbench, so the next several sections introduce the core components one at a time, alongside the schematic symbol used to represent each one.

The Core Components: Symbols and Functions

Every circuit, no matter how complex, is built from a small number of fundamental component types. This section introduces five of them: the resistor, capacitor, diode, transistor, and LED. Each has its own schematic symbol, its own job, and its own reason for appearing in the StarterBot's electronics.

A resistor is a component that opposes the flow of current by a fixed, known amount, measured in ohms, and used to limit current or divide voltage in a circuit. You already used resistance conceptually in Chapter 6's Ohm's Law calculations; a resistor is simply a physical part manufactured to provide a specific, reliable resistance value. Resistors are color-coded with bands of paint indicating value and tolerance, letting you identify a resistor's value without a multimeter once you learn the code.

A capacitor is a component that stores electrical energy in an electric field between two conductive plates, and releases that energy back into the circuit when needed. Unlike a battery, which stores energy chemically and releases it slowly over minutes or hours, a capacitor stores a much smaller amount of energy but can charge and discharge almost instantly. On a robot's control board, small capacitors smooth out sudden dips or spikes in voltage, for example when a motor first switches on and briefly pulls extra current.

A diode is a component that allows current to flow in only one direction and blocks it in the other, acting like a one-way valve for electricity. This property makes diodes useful for protecting circuits from being connected backward, and for converting alternating current into direct current in a power supply. A diode has two terminals, the anode and the cathode, and current flows only from anode to cathode, never the reverse.

Before moving on to the two remaining components, it helps to see these first three side by side, since their symbols are easy to confuse at a glance:

Component Symbol Shape Primary Function
Resistor Zig-zag line or plain rectangle Limits current to a fixed, known amount
Capacitor Two parallel lines with a small gap Stores energy briefly in an electric field
Diode Triangle pointing into a flat bar Allows current to flow in one direction only

A transistor is a component that uses a small input current or voltage to control a much larger current flowing through it, functioning as either an electronic switch or an amplifier. Transistors are the building blocks of every microcontroller and every motor driver you will meet later in this chapter, because they let a low-power signal from a control chip switch on and off a high-power connection to a motor or a light, without the low-power signal ever touching the high-power current directly.

An LED (light-emitting diode) is a type of diode that emits visible light when current flows through it in the forward direction. An LED is a diode first and a light source second: it shares the diode's one-way current behavior, but it also converts a portion of that current directly into light rather than heat, which makes it far more efficient than an incandescent bulb. Every LED requires a resistor in series with it to limit current, since an LED's own resistance is too low to safely limit current on its own; connecting an LED directly to a battery without a resistor will typically burn it out within seconds.

Because these five components appear on nearly every circuit board, being able to recognize each one by its schematic symbol, without hesitating, is a foundational skill for everything that follows in this chapter.

Powering the Circuit: Battery and Power Supply

Every circuit needs an energy source, and robotics projects typically draw on one of two kinds: a battery or a power supply. A battery is a device that stores chemical energy and converts it to electrical energy on demand, delivering a roughly steady voltage until its stored energy runs low. The StarterBot's single 12V battery, introduced in Chapter 1 and referenced throughout Chapter 6, is a rechargeable battery pack built from multiple cells connected internally to reach 12 volts.

A power supply is a device that converts electrical energy from one form or voltage to another, usually converting a wall outlet's alternating current into the steady direct current voltage a circuit requires. Unlike a battery, a power supply does not store its own energy; it depends on being plugged into an external source and simply transforms that source's voltage and current into a usable form. Many robotics workbenches use a benchtop power supply during testing, since it can supply a precise, adjustable voltage without draining a battery, and it can be switched off instantly if something goes wrong.

The following table summarizes the practical differences a robotics student needs to keep in mind when choosing between them:

Feature Battery Power Supply
Energy source Stores chemical energy internally Converts external outlet power
Portability Portable, works untethered Requires a wall connection
Runtime Limited, depletes with use Unlimited while plugged in
Typical robotics use Powering the robot during operation Bench testing before final assembly

Both must ultimately connect to the rest of the circuit through a control point that lets current flow only when intended, which is the job of the next two components.

Controlling the Flow: Switch and Relay

A switch is a mechanical device that opens or closes a circuit by making or breaking a physical electrical connection, controlled directly by a person's hand. The simplest switch, a single-pole single-throw (SPST) switch, has exactly two positions: fully open (no connection, no current) and fully closed (connection made, current flows). More complex switches can route current to one of several paths, but every switch shares the same basic job: a physical, human-operated gate for current.

A relay is an electrically operated switch that uses a small control current to open or close a separate, often much larger, circuit. Inside a relay, a small electromagnet pulls a mechanical arm to close (or open) a set of contacts, similar to the electromagnetic effects you may have encountered when studying motors. This design solves a problem a plain switch cannot: a microcontroller's output pins can only safely supply a tiny current, far too little to directly power something like a large motor or a household appliance, but that tiny current is more than enough to energize a relay's electromagnet, which then switches the much larger current on the relay's other side.

Relays and transistor-based motor drivers (covered later in this chapter) solve the same underlying problem—letting a weak control signal command a strong load—but they do it differently:

  • A relay uses a mechanical, moving contact, which makes an audible click and can wear out over many cycles, but can switch very high currents and works with both AC and DC loads.
  • A transistor-based motor driver has no moving parts, switches almost instantly, and is generally preferred for the fast, frequent switching a robot's drive motors need.

Breadboard: Prototyping Without Solder

Now that you can identify the core components and the power sources that drive them, it is time to actually connect them into a working circuit—without committing to a permanent solder joint on your first attempt. A breadboard is a reusable prototyping board with a grid of spring-loaded holes that let components and wires be inserted and removed without soldering, allowing circuits to be built, tested, and modified quickly.

Internally, a breadboard's holes are connected in hidden strips beneath the plastic surface:

  • Each short row of five holes in the center section is electrically connected, so inserting a component's leg anywhere in that row of five connects it to everything else in that same row.
  • The long rows running along the top and bottom edges, usually marked with a red (+) and blue (-) stripe, are power rails, connected the entire length of the board, used to distribute power and ground to many components at once.
  • The center gap running down the middle of the board is not connected across, which is exactly where a chip or component that needs pins on both sides is placed.

Because no solder is involved, a mistake on a breadboard costs nothing more than the time it takes to pull a wire out and reposition it, which is exactly why breadboards are the standard first step for testing any new circuit design, including the LED-resistor-battery circuit from the diagram earlier in this chapter.

Soldering: Making Permanent Connections

A breadboard is perfect for testing, but its spring-loaded connections can loosen with vibration, which is a real concern on a robot that drives, bumps, and vibrates during operation. Soldering is the process of joining two metal parts, typically a component lead and a circuit board pad, by melting a metal alloy (solder) that cools into a strong, permanent electrical and mechanical connection. Once a circuit design has been tested and confirmed on a breadboard, soldering is how that design becomes a durable, vibration-resistant part of the finished robot.

A basic soldering procedure follows a consistent sequence:

  1. Heat the soldering iron to the correct temperature (typically 300–370°C for electronics work) and let it stabilize.
  2. Clean the iron's tip on a damp sponge or brass wool to remove old, oxidized solder.
  3. Position the component leads through the board holes and hold the iron tip against both the lead and the pad simultaneously.
  4. Feed solder wire into the joint, not directly onto the iron tip, letting heat from the joint itself melt the solder.
  5. Remove the solder and then the iron, and let the joint cool undisturbed for several seconds.
  6. Inspect the finished joint: it should look shiny and shaped like a small volcano, not dull, cracked, or blobby.

!!! danger "Soldering iron safety" A soldering iron tip reaches temperatures well above the boiling point of water and stays hot for minutes after being unplugged. Always return the iron to its stand when not actively soldering, keep the cord clear of the tip, and never test whether the iron is hot by touching it directly.

Multimeter: Measuring and Testing

Whether a circuit lives on a breadboard or has been soldered permanently, you eventually need a way to confirm it is actually behaving the way your diagram predicted. A multimeter is a handheld instrument that measures multiple electrical properties, typically voltage, current, and resistance, selectable by a mode dial or button, used to test and troubleshoot circuits. A multimeter is the primary diagnostic tool for verifying the calculations you practiced in Chapter 6 against a real, physical circuit.

Each mode on a multimeter measures a different quantity and requires a different connection technique:

Mode Measures How to Connect Common Use
Voltage (V) Potential difference between two points Probes touch two points in parallel, circuit stays connected Checking battery charge, confirming a power rail voltage
Current (A) Rate of charge flow through a path Meter placed in series, circuit must be opened to insert it Confirming a motor is drawing expected current
Resistance (Ω) Opposition to current in an unpowered component Probes touch two ends of a component removed from a powered circuit Confirming a resistor's actual value
Continuity Whether a path is electrically connected Probes touch two points, meter beeps if resistance is near zero Checking a wire or solder joint for a broken connection

!!! warning "Never measure current like you measure voltage" Voltage mode is connected in parallel and current mode is connected in series, and confusing the two is one of the most common multimeter mistakes. Placing a multimeter in current mode directly across a battery's terminals, the way you would for a voltage reading, creates a near-zero-resistance path through the meter itself and can blow the meter's internal fuse or damage the battery.

Printed Circuit Board

Breadboards and hand-soldered connections both work well for one-off projects and classroom prototypes, but neither scales well to a product built many times over. A printed circuit board (PCB) is a flat board made of insulating material with thin copper traces etched onto its surface, replacing individual wires with permanent, precisely placed conductive paths that connect components soldered onto the board. Where a breadboard's connections live in reusable spring clips and a hand-soldered protoboard relies on point-to-point wires, a PCB's traces are designed in software ahead of time and manufactured identically every time.

The tradeoffs among these three approaches to physically realizing a circuit follow a clear pattern:

Approach Reusable? Reliable Under Vibration? Best Suited For
Breadboard Yes, fully reusable No, connections can loosen Early testing and quick iteration
Hand-soldered protoboard No, permanent Yes, solid joints One-off finished projects
Printed circuit board No, permanent Yes, most robust Repeated manufacturing, final products

A control board like the microcontroller you programmed in Chapter 4 is itself built on a PCB, with its resistors, capacitors, and connectors all soldered onto copper traces that were designed and manufactured long before you ever opened the box.

Motor Driver: Controlling the StarterBot's Motors

Recall from Chapter 4 that a program commands a Yellow Jacket DC gearmotor with a power level, a fraction between full reverse and full forward such as 0.6, and that Chapter 1 described these same gearmotors as the drivetrain for the StarterBot's wheels. Sending a fractional power command from a microcontroller only works, however, because of a component that sits physically between the microcontroller and the motor. A motor driver is a circuit, typically built around transistors, that takes a low-power control signal from a microcontroller and uses it to switch a motor's much higher current and voltage on, off, or in reverse.

A microcontroller's output pins, like a relay's control input, can only safely supply a few milliamps of current, while a Yellow Jacket gearmotor under load can draw several amps, roughly a thousand times more. A motor driver bridges that gap using an arrangement of transistors called an H-bridge, which can do something a simple relay cannot do easily: reverse the motor's direction electronically, without any moving mechanical parts, simply by switching which pair of transistors conducts.

  • Setting the transistors one way lets current flow through the motor in the forward direction, spinning it forward.
  • Setting the transistors the opposite way reverses current through the motor, spinning it in reverse.
  • Rapidly switching the transistors on and off many times per second, a technique called pulse-width modulation, controls the effective power level, which is exactly how a program's "run at 0.6 power" command becomes a real, adjustable motor speed rather than only a full-on or full-off command.

Without a motor driver, a microcontroller could only ever fully connect or fully disconnect a motor from the battery, producing the same simple full-on behavior you first commanded in Chapter 4; the motor driver is what makes variable speed and reversible direction possible at all.

Circuit Simulation and Circuit Design

Every component and skill in this chapter—reading a diagram, recognizing symbols, wiring a breadboard, soldering, measuring with a multimeter—comes together in a single overall process: designing a circuit that solves a specific problem, before ever touching a physical part. Circuit design is the process of selecting components and specifying their connections to build a circuit that meets a set of requirements, such as lighting an LED at a safe current or driving a motor at a target speed.

A well-organized circuit design process moves through a predictable sequence of stages:

  1. Define the requirement (for example, "light one LED safely from a 12V battery").
  2. Select components that can meet the requirement (an LED with a known forward voltage, a resistor sized to limit current with Ohm's Law).
  3. Draw a circuit diagram showing how the components connect.
  4. Simulate the design to confirm it behaves as expected.
  5. Build the circuit, first on a breadboard and then, once confirmed, soldered or on a PCB.
  6. Test the built circuit with a multimeter to verify it matches the simulation and the original requirement.

Step 4 depends on a tool this chapter has not yet named directly. Circuit simulation is the use of software to model a circuit's electrical behavior mathematically, predicting voltages, currents, and component states before any physical parts are purchased or assembled. Circuit simulation software, such as Tinkercad Circuits or LTspice, applies the same equations you learned in Chapter 6, including Ohm's Law, to a diagram you build on screen, then reports whether the design will work, whether a component might be overloaded, and what its actual voltage and current values would be.

Simulating a design first offers a clear practical advantage: a mistake in simulation costs nothing, while the same mistake in a physical circuit can damage an LED, a transistor, or a battery, and cost both money and time to diagnose. This is precisely why professional engineers, not just students, simulate a design before ordering parts or manufacturing a PCB.

This design-simulate-build-test cycle is the one you will return to for the rest of this course, whether the circuit ahead lights a single LED or drives every motor on a finished robot. You now have the vocabulary, from resistor to relay, and the practical skills, from breadboarding to multimeter testing, to move confidently between a circuit diagram on a screen and a working circuit on your bench.
