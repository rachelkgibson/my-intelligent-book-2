Sensors and Measurement

Summary

Students already know what a sensor and an actuator are from programming their robot. This chapter goes much deeper: precision measurement, direct and indirect measurement techniques, coordinate systems, and a tour of specific sensor types (light, temperature, distance, touch, sound, encoders, potentiometers). The chapter closes with sensor calibration, data transmission, and signal processing, preparing students to select and tune the right sensor for any task.

From "It Has a Sensor" to "It Knows the Distance"

Back in Chapter 4, you defined a sensor as any component that converts a physical quantity—light, distance, temperature, sound—into an electrical signal your microcontroller can read. You wrote a few lines of code to read a sensor value and use it to command an actuator, the component that turns an electrical signal back into motion or another physical effect. That was enough to make a robot react to the world.

This chapter asks a harder question: how good is that reaction? A robot that merely knows "something is nearby" behaves very differently from one that knows "the wall is 14.2 centimeters away, and that reading is accurate to within 2 millimeters." The gap between those two robots is the entire subject of this chapter. You will learn how to measure precisely, how to locate objects in space using a coordinate system, how to choose among the specific sensor types your kit provides, how to calibrate a sensor so its numbers can be trusted, and how to move that data cleanly from the sensor to the microcontroller's brain.

By the end of the chapter, you will stop thinking of a sensor as a single component and start thinking of it as the first link in a measurement pipeline—one that can introduce error at every stage, and one you now have the tools to control.

Precision Measurement: Why "Good Enough" Isn't Always Good Enough

Every measurement a sensor produces is an estimate, not a perfect fact. Precision measurement is the practice of obtaining sensor readings that are consistent, fine-grained, and trustworthy enough to support the decision the robot must make. Precision is not the same thing as accuracy: a sensor can report the same distance reading five times in a row (high precision) while still being wrong every time (low accuracy), because it was never calibrated against a known value.

Consider two robots approaching the same wall:

  • Robot A's distance sensor reports "close" or "far"—a coarse, two-state reading good enough to stop before a wall.
  • Robot B's distance sensor reports a number in millimeters, refreshed 20 times per second, accurate to within 1% of the true distance.

Robot A can avoid a collision. Robot B can park itself exactly 5 centimeters from a wall, follow a wall at a constant offset, or dock precisely with a charging station. The difference is not the sensor hardware alone—it is how precisely the robot is willing to measure, process, and trust the data that sensor produces.

Precision matters more as tasks get harder. A line-following robot needs enough precision to distinguish the edge of a black line from ordinary floor noise. A robotic arm stacking blocks needs enough precision to place a block within a few millimeters of its target, or the stack topples. Whenever you design a sensing system, ask what level of precision the task actually requires—overspending on precision wastes processing time and money, but underspending causes failures that are hard to diagnose later.

Direct Measurement vs. Indirect Measurement

Sensors do not all obtain their readings the same way. Direct measurement occurs when a sensor's output corresponds straightforwardly to the physical quantity being measured, with little or no calculation required. Indirect measurement occurs when the physical quantity of interest cannot be sensed directly, so the robot measures a related quantity and calculates the value it actually needs.

A touch sensor is a clean example of direct measurement: pressing the switch closes a circuit, and the microcontroller reads a simple "pressed" or "not pressed" signal that directly represents the physical event. A distance sensor that uses sound, by contrast, is a classic case of indirect measurement: the sensor cannot detect "distance" as a raw physical quantity. Instead, it measures the time an ultrasonic pulse takes to travel to an object and echo back, and the robot calculates distance using the known speed of sound.

This distinction matters because indirect measurements inherit every error in the calculation used to produce them. If the speed-of-sound constant used in a distance calculation is wrong for the current air temperature, every distance reading will be off by the same proportional amount, even though the raw timing measurement was fine. The following table reinforces the distinction with sensors covered later in this chapter.

Sensor What it directly senses What is reported Direct or indirect?
Touch sensor Circuit open/closed Pressed / not pressed Direct
Potentiometer Wiper position (resistance) Angle or position value Direct
Encoder Light or magnetic pulses per rotation Rotation count, speed Indirect (pulses converted to angle/speed)
Ultrasonic distance sensor Echo travel time Distance in cm Indirect (time converted using speed of sound)
Temperature sensor (thermistor) Resistance change Temperature in °C Indirect (resistance converted using a formula)

Notice that several of the sensors your robot already relies on for "simple" jobs are secretly performing a calculation behind the scenes. Keeping that calculation in mind will help you understand later, in the section on sensor calibration, why some sensors drift out of accuracy more easily than others.

Coordinate Systems: Giving the Robot a Sense of Space

Knowing a distance or an angle is only useful if the robot also knows what point in space that number is measured from and in what direction. A coordinate system is a framework of reference axes and an origin point that lets a robot describe the position and orientation of itself or of objects around it using numbers. Without a coordinate system, "10 centimeters" is a meaningless fraction—10 centimeters from where, and in which direction?

Robots commonly use more than one coordinate system at the same time, and part of good sensor design is keeping straight which one a given reading belongs to:

  • A robot-centered (egocentric) coordinate system places the origin at the robot itself, usually at its center or its front sensor mount. Distances and angles are measured relative to the robot's own body—"the wall is 30 cm ahead and 10 degrees to the left."
  • A world (global) coordinate system places the origin at a fixed point in the environment, such as the corner of a room or a competition field. Positions are measured relative to that fixed point, so the robot's location changes as it moves, but the origin never does.
  • A polar coordinate system describes a point using a distance and an angle from an origin, which is a natural match for many rotating sensors, such as a distance sensor mounted on a spinning turret.
  • A Cartesian coordinate system describes a point using perpendicular axes (x, y, and sometimes z), which is a natural match for grid-based navigation and mapping.

A signpost worth remembering: every sensor reading is meaningless until you know which coordinate system it belongs to, and converting between systems (for example, from a sensor's egocentric polar reading to the robot's position on a global Cartesian map) is one of the core calculations in robot navigation, revisited in later chapters on control systems.

A Tour of Sensor Types

With precision, measurement type, and coordinate systems established as the underlying concepts, the rest of this chapter surveys the specific sensors most robotics kits provide. Each sensor converts a different physical quantity into an electrical signal, and each is suited to a different job.

Light Sensor

A light sensor detects the intensity of visible or infrared light striking it and reports that intensity as a voltage or digital value. The most common type in educational robotics kits is a photoresistor or phototransistor, whose electrical resistance changes depending on how much light reaches it. Robots use light sensors to follow a line printed on the floor (the line reflects less light than the surrounding surface), to detect whether a room is dark or bright, or to find a light source by comparing readings from two sensors mounted apart.

Temperature Sensor

A temperature sensor converts ambient or contact temperature into an electrical signal, typically by using a thermistor (a resistor whose resistance changes predictably with temperature) or an integrated circuit sensor that outputs a voltage proportional to temperature. This is a textbook example of indirect measurement: the sensor measures resistance or voltage, and the microcontroller applies a formula to convert that value into degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit. Robots use temperature sensors to monitor motor overheating, check whether a room is safe for entry, or trigger a cooling fan.

Distance Sensor

A distance sensor measures the space between the sensor and the nearest object in its detection path. The two most common technologies in educational kits are ultrasonic sensors, which time an echoing sound pulse, and infrared sensors, which measure the angle or intensity of reflected infrared light. Distance sensors are central to obstacle avoidance, wall-following, and parking behaviors, and—because most types rely on indirect measurement—they are especially sensitive to calibration error, a topic covered later in this chapter.

Touch Sensor

A touch sensor (also called a bump or contact sensor) detects physical contact by closing or opening an electrical circuit when pressed. It is the simplest sensor in this tour and a direct-measurement device: no calculation converts its reading into a physical quantity, because "pressed" is the physical quantity. Robots use touch sensors as bump switches on a chassis edge, as limit switches that stop an arm at the end of its range of motion, and as simple user input buttons.

Sound Sensor

A sound sensor converts air pressure vibrations (sound waves) into an electrical signal, usually using a small microphone element followed by an amplifier circuit. Simple sound sensors report only volume (loudness), while more advanced versions can detect specific frequencies or claps. Robots use sound sensors to respond to a clap or whistle command, to detect an alarm, or to react to the volume of ambient noise.

Encoder

An encoder measures the rotation of a shaft, most often a motor shaft or wheel axle, by counting pulses generated as the shaft turns, using slotted disks and light sensors or magnets and magnetic sensors. Encoders answer the question "how far, or how fast, has this wheel turned?"—information a robot needs to drive a precise distance, maintain a target speed, or estimate its own position by counting wheel rotations. This is another indirect measurement: the raw signal is a stream of pulses, and software converts pulse count into rotation angle or distance traveled using the known pulses-per-revolution of the encoder disk.

Potentiometer

A potentiometer is a variable resistor whose resistance changes as a knob, dial, or lever attached to it is rotated or slid, producing an output voltage that directly represents its physical position. Because the output voltage corresponds straightforwardly to position, a potentiometer is a direct-measurement sensor. Robots use potentiometers to sense the angle of a steering mechanism, the position of a joint on a robotic arm, or as a simple manual input control for adjusting a setting like speed or volume.

Sensor Calibration: Making Readings Trustworthy

A sensor fresh out of the box rarely reports a physical quantity with perfect accuracy. Sensor calibration is the process of comparing a sensor's raw output against a known, trusted reference value and adjusting the sensor's readings (or the formula that converts them) so that its output matches reality as closely as possible. Calibration is what turns "the sensor reads 480" into "the sensor reads 30 centimeters," and what corrects for the fact that no two sensors of the same model behave identically.

Calibration matters for a specific, practical reason: manufacturing variation. Two distance sensors of the exact same model, purchased at the same time, may report slightly different raw values for the exact same distance, because of tiny differences introduced during manufacturing. Environmental conditions add a second layer of drift—temperature, humidity, and even battery voltage can shift a sensor's baseline reading over the course of a single class period.

A typical calibration procedure for a distance sensor follows these steps:

  1. Place a known object at a precisely measured distance from the sensor (for example, exactly 20 cm, using a ruler).
  2. Record the sensor's raw output value at that known distance.
  3. Repeat at several other known distances (for example, 10 cm, 30 cm, and 50 cm) to capture how the raw value changes across the sensor's range.
  4. Use the recorded pairs of (raw value, true distance) to build a conversion formula or lookup table.
  5. Apply that formula in code so future raw readings are automatically converted into accurate distance values.
  6. Re-calibrate periodically, especially after changing batteries or operating in a new environment.

!!! tip A sensor that is precise but never calibrated will confidently report the wrong answer every single time. Precision without calibration is a trap—always calibrate against a known reference before trusting a sensor's absolute value.

From Sensor to Microcontroller: Data Transmission and Signal Processing

A calibrated sensor reading is not automatically useful the instant it is captured—it must travel from the sensor to the microcontroller and be cleaned up along the way. Data transmission is the process of moving a sensor's signal from the sensor itself to the microcontroller that will interpret it, using an electrical connection and an agreed-upon signaling method. Signal processing is the set of techniques used to clean, filter, or transform a raw signal so that noise, drift, and irrelevant variation are reduced before the microcontroller acts on the data.

Data transmission can take several forms, and the choice affects how much signal processing is needed downstream:

  • Analog transmission sends a continuously varying voltage (for example, 0 to 5 volts) that the microcontroller must convert into a digital number using an analog-to-digital converter. Potentiometers and many light sensors use analog transmission.
  • Digital transmission sends discrete on/off pulses or structured digital messages (using protocols such as I2C or SPI) that the microcontroller reads directly as numbers, with no conversion step. Many modern distance sensors and encoders use digital transmission.
  • Pulse-based transmission sends information encoded as the timing or count of pulses, as with an ultrasonic sensor's echo pulse or an encoder's rotation pulses; the microcontroller must measure pulse timing or count pulses to extract the value.

Once a signal arrives at the microcontroller, signal processing techniques are commonly applied before the value is trusted:

  • Averaging (smoothing) combines several recent readings into one value, reducing the effect of random noise, as explored in the calibration MicroSim above.
  • Filtering removes specific unwanted patterns from a signal, such as a low-pass filter that suppresses rapid flickers while preserving slower, meaningful changes.
  • Thresholding converts a continuous value into a simple decision, such as treating any light-sensor reading below a set value as "dark."
  • Debouncing prevents a single physical event—like pressing a touch sensor—from being misread as several rapid presses due to mechanical vibration when the switch contacts touch.

Chapter Summary

Precision measurement, direct and indirect measurement, and coordinate systems form the conceptual foundation for everything a sensor reports. The seven sensor types toured in this chapter—light, temperature, distance, touch, sound, encoder, and potentiometer—cover the great majority of sensing tasks in educational robotics, and each fits somewhere on the direct-versus-indirect spectrum you now know how to identify. Sensor calibration turns a raw, sensor-specific number into a trustworthy physical measurement, and understanding data transmission and signal processing explains what happens to that measurement on its short but important journey to the microcontroller. The next time you write read_sensor() in your robot's code, you will know exactly what has to go right before that single number can be trusted.

??? note "Quick check: Is a photoresistor-based light sensor a direct or indirect measurement device? Click to expand" Indirect. The sensor directly measures a change in electrical resistance caused by light, and the microcontroller converts that resistance value into a light-intensity reading using a known relationship—so a calculation stands between the raw signal and the reported quantity.
