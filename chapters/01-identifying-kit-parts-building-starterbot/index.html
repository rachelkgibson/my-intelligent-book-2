Identifying Kit Parts and Building Your StarterBot

Summary

This chapter opens the course with a real robot kit in hand: the goBILDA FTC StarterBot Kit. Students first learn to identify the structural hardware, motion components, power/motor parts, and fasteners that make up the kit, then read and follow a guided assembly manual to build a complete robot. By the end of this chapter, students will have a working robot chassis in front of them and firsthand experience with the vocabulary and physical components that every later chapter builds on.

Robot Kit Overview

Most robotics courses start with theory: circuits, forces, and code, followed months later by a real machine. This course flips that order. Before you learn why a motor spins or how a program controls a servo, you are going to open a box, spread out the parts, and build a real, drivable robot. The theory will make a lot more sense later once your hands already know what a gear, a shaft, and a chassis feel like.

The kit you will build from is the goBILDA FTC StarterBot Kit (SKU 3200-4008-2627), a robotics kit designed for the preseason of FIRST Tech Challenge (FTC), a national robotics competition for middle and high school teams. Unlike a toy kit with a dozen snap-together pieces, the StarterBot Kit ships with hundreds of individual aluminum, plastic, and metal components, along with a 20-page assembly manual that walks you through 40 numbered build steps. When you finish, you will have a rolling robot chassis with a working drivetrain, a battery mount, and an intake mechanism, the same kind of subsystem an FTC team uses as the backbone of a competition robot.

Every part in the kit falls into one of four broad categories, and learning to sort parts into these categories is the very first skill this chapter teaches:

  • Structural hardware — the aluminum channels, mounts, and plates that form the robot's frame, or "skeleton"
  • Motion hardware — the shafts, hubs, bearings, gears, sprockets, chain, and wheels that let the robot move
  • Power and motor components — the battery, motors, and servos that provide energy and rotational force
  • Fasteners and hardware — the screws, nuts, washers, and hex keys that hold everything together

!!! tip Don't worry about memorizing every part name today. This chapter builds your vocabulary the same way a mechanic builds theirs: by handling real parts, matching them to a parts list, and using them right away in a real build.

Kit Parts Identification

Before any assembly manual makes sense, you need to be able to identify parts. Every professional assembly guide, from furniture instructions to aircraft maintenance manuals, begins with a Bill of Materials (BOM), a complete list of every part needed, usually shown with a picture, a part name, and a quantity. The StarterBot Kit's BOM lists every U-channel, shaft, gear, motor, and screw that ships in the box, organized by category.

Learning to read a BOM is a transferable skill you will use throughout your career in any technical field: you scan the picture, compare it to the part in your hand, check the quantity, and set it aside in a labeled tray or bag. Skipping this step is the single most common reason first-time builders get stuck at step 15 of a manual, only to discover they used the wrong length of shaft back at step 3.

Instead of memorizing the whole BOM from a page of tiny thumbnail images, it helps to explore an exploded view of the finished robot, where each region of the machine is linked back to its hardware category. Clicking through this kind of exploded diagram lets you build a mental map of "where does this type of part usually go" before you ever pick up a screwdriver.

Structural Hardware

Structural hardware refers to the rigid parts that give the robot its shape and hold every other component in a fixed position relative to the rest of the machine. If motion hardware is the robot's muscles and power components are its energy source, structural hardware is its skeleton.

The most common piece of structural hardware in the StarterBot Kit is the U-channel: a length of extruded aluminum bent into a U-shaped cross-section, with a regular pattern of holes drilled along its length for mounting screws. U-channels are named by how many holes run along their length, which also tells you roughly how long the piece is. A 1-Hole U-Channel is a short stub used as a spacer or bracket, while a 15-Hole U-Channel is long enough to form an entire side rail of the chassis.

The following table lists the U-channel sizes used in the StarterBot build, since recognizing these by hole count is a skill you will rely on for every future robotics project, not just this kit.

U-Channel Type Approx. Length Typical Use in the Build
1-Hole U-Channel Shortest Small brackets and spacers
1-Hole Low U-Channel Shortest, shallower profile Tight-clearance brackets
3-Hole Mini U-Channel Short Small mounting brackets
8-Hole U-Channel Medium Cross braces and short rails
10-Hole Low U-Channel Medium, shallower profile Low-profile chassis rails
15-Hole U-Channel Longest Main chassis side rails

Beyond U-channels, the kit includes other structural parts that connect channels to one another or to motion hardware:

  • Quad Block Mount — a block with four screw points, used to join two U-channels at a corner or to mount a bearing
  • Dual Block Mount — a smaller block with two screw points, used for lighter connections
  • Drop-Center Bearing Plate — a flat plate with a lowered center section that holds a bearing in perfect alignment with a shaft passing through the frame

Because structural hardware carries no moving parts of its own, it is the easiest category to identify: if a part is solid aluminum, has a repeating hole pattern, and does not spin or roll, it is almost certainly structural hardware.

Motion Hardware Components

Motion hardware is the collection of parts that transmits rotational force from a motor to the wheels, or otherwise allows a part of the robot to move. This is the most mechanically complex category in the kit, because motion hardware parts must fit together with very tight tolerances to spin freely without wobbling.

At the center of the StarterBot's motion system is the 8mm REX Shaft, a precision-ground steel shaft available in several lengths that passes through the frame and carries rotating parts along its length. A shaft alone cannot transmit force to anything; it needs a way to grip a wheel, sprocket, or gear so the two rotate together. That job belongs to hubs: the 8mm REX Sonic Hub and 8mm REX Hyper Hub both clamp onto the shaft and provide a mounting pattern that other motion parts bolt onto.

For a shaft to spin freely through a hole in a U-channel or bearing plate without grinding against the aluminum, it passes through an 8mm REX Bearing, a ring of hardened steel with an inner race that lets the shaft rotate with almost no friction. Builders also use thin 0.5mm shims to fine-tune the spacing between parts on a shaft, and collars to lock a part's exact position on the shaft so it cannot slide side to side.

Once a shaft is supported by bearings and fitted with hubs, it can drive a wheel directly, or it can drive another shaft through a chain-and-sprocket or gear-and-gear connection. The StarterBot Kit uses both approaches:

  1. Chain-and-sprocket drivetrain — a 10-Tooth Sprocket on the motor's output shaft is connected by plastic chain (snapped together from individual links into a closed loop) to a larger sprocket on the wheel shaft, multiplying torque while reducing speed.
  2. Gear-driven mechanisms — the 30-Tooth Gear, 20-Tooth Gear, and 100-Tooth Gear mesh directly, tooth to tooth, to transmit rotation between shafts without the flexibility of a chain, which is useful in the kit's intake roller mechanism.
  3. Wheels — the finished drivetrain rolls on a mix of wheel types: 72mm and 48mm GripForce Gecko Wheels for high-traction driving, Omni Wheels that roll freely sideways for smooth turning, and Hogback Wheels that support the frame without adding drive traction.

Notice the pattern in every motion assembly: a shaft is supported by bearings, positioned with shims and collars, and driven by a hub, gear, or sprocket. Once you can recognize that pattern, every motion subassembly in the manual, and in future robots you build, will look familiar rather than overwhelming.

Power and Motor Components

Structural and motion hardware only matter once something makes them move. Power and motor components are the parts that store and convert electrical energy into rotational force. The StarterBot Kit runs on a single 12V battery, held in place by a Battery Mount and secured with a Battery Strap so it cannot shift during a match or a bumpy test drive.

The kit includes two models of Yellow Jacket motor, a type of DC gearmotor that combines an electric motor with an internal gearbox. The gearbox trades rotational speed for torque (twisting force), and the two ratios included in the kit sit at opposite ends of that trade-off.

Motor Gear Ratio Speed Torque Typical Use
Yellow Jacket 19.2:1 Lower ratio Faster rotation Lower torque Driving wheels for quick movement
Yellow Jacket 50.9:1 Higher ratio Slower rotation Higher torque Mechanisms needing more twisting force, like an intake roller

In addition to the two DC motors, the kit includes Speed Servos, a different kind of motor that rotates to a specific commanded angle rather than spinning continuously. Servos mount inside a Compact ServoBlock, a housing that also holds a wheel hub, so the servo can be geared to drive a wheel directly. You will meet servos again in a later chapter on control systems, but for now it is enough to recognize a servo as the small, boxy motor bolted inside its own block housing, distinct from the larger cylindrical Yellow Jacket gearmotors.

Fasteners and Hardware

Every structural and motion part in the kit stays together because of fasteners: the screws, nuts, and washers that clamp parts to one another. Fasteners are easy to overlook because they are small, but a build with the wrong fastener in the wrong hole will fail, either falling apart during a match or binding so tightly that a shaft cannot spin.

The StarterBot Kit uses three sizes of socket head screw (8mm, 10mm, and 12mm), named for the length of the screw's shaft, and driven with a hex key that fits into a hexagonal socket in the screw's head. Paired with each screw is typically a locknut, a nut with a nylon insert that grips the screw's threads so vibration cannot loosen it over time, and often a washer, a flat disc that spreads the clamping force over a wider area so the aluminum channel is not dented or cracked.

Here is a quick reference for matching each fastener to its role in the build:

  • 8mm socket head screw — shortest length, used to join thin stacks of parts such as two U-channels
  • 10mm socket head screw — medium length, used for block mounts and bearing plates
  • 12mm socket head screw — longest length, used where several parts stack together, such as a motor mount plus a channel
  • Locknut — threads onto a screw and resists loosening from vibration
  • Washer — spreads clamping force and protects the aluminum surface
  • Hex key — an L-shaped tool that fits the socket in each screw head; the manual specifies which size hex key to use for each screw

!!! warning A locknut that spins too easily by hand has lost its nylon grip and should be replaced. A locknut that requires heavy force from the very first turn is being cross-threaded. Both situations are worth stopping to fix before continuing the build.

Assembly Instructions Reading

Now that you can identify every category of part, the next skill is learning to read a technical assembly manual, the same skill an aircraft mechanic, furniture installer, or robotics engineer relies on daily. The StarterBot manual presents 40 numbered steps, each showing an illustration of the parts needed, an arrow or highlight showing where they attach, and a callout list naming each part and how many are required.

Reading an assembly manual is not the same as reading a paragraph of text top to bottom. Instead, experienced builders repeat a small cycle for every single step, checking their work before moving on rather than plowing ahead and hoping for the best. Because this cycle repeats identically at every one of the 40 steps, it is worth slowing down to see it as its own process, separate from any single step's specific parts.

Two habits separate a fast, frustration-free build from a slow, error-prone one: reading the entire step before touching a single part, and verifying each step before moving to the next. Skipping verification is how a shim gets left out three steps before it becomes impossible to reach.

Guided Robot Assembly

With the reading cycle established, the StarterBot manual's 40 steps group naturally into a handful of larger construction phases. Recognizing these phases helps you understand where you are in the build and what the current step is building toward, rather than experiencing the manual as 40 disconnected instructions.

  1. Frame construction — U-channels are joined using block mounts and standoffs to form the rectangular base and side rails of the chassis, establishing the skeleton every other part attaches to.
  2. Shaft and bearing subassemblies — 8mm REX Shafts are threaded through bearings pressed into the frame, with shims and collars used to set exact spacing, and hubs added to prepare each shaft to receive a wheel, gear, or sprocket.
  3. Chain-and-sprocket and gear drivetrain — sprockets and gears are bolted to the hubs on their shafts, plastic chain links are snapped into closed loops, and the loops are looped over paired sprockets to transmit motor rotation to the wheels.
  4. Drop-center bearing plates — these plates are bolted across the frame to hold the drivetrain shafts in precise alignment so nothing binds or wobbles once the frame is closed up.
  5. Battery mount — the Battery Mount and Battery Strap are installed in a protected, centrally balanced location on the chassis.
  6. Intake roller mechanism — a gear-driven roller subassembly is built separately and then bolted onto the front of the chassis.
  7. Wheels — Gecko Wheels are mounted through servo-driven Compact ServoBlocks, and Omni Wheels and Hogback Wheels are bolted onto their prepared shafts, completing the rolling chassis.

Notice that phases 2 through 4 all revolve around getting shafts to spin smoothly and stay aligned: this is the mechanical heart of the robot, and it is worth double-checking spacing and tightness in these steps even if the manual does not explicitly say to pause and verify.

Robot Subassembly Build

A subassembly is a smaller group of parts built and tested as its own unit before it is attached to the main chassis. Manuals for anything mechanically complex, from furniture to aircraft to robots, rely heavily on subassemblies because it is far easier to fix a mistake in a small group of six parts sitting on a workbench than to fix the same mistake after it has been buried inside a fully assembled machine.

The StarterBot manual has you build several subassemblies before they ever touch the main frame: a shaft-bearing-hub subassembly, a sprocket-and-chain drivetrain subassembly, a battery mount subassembly, an intake roller subassembly, and a wheel-and-ServoBlock subassembly for each Gecko wheel. Recognizing which parts belong together as a subassembly, before the manual tells you explicitly, is a skill that becomes more valuable as robots grow more complex in later chapters.

To check how well you can sort real StarterBot parts into their correct subassembly group, work through the matching activity below. It reuses the same part names from the Bill of Materials you explored earlier in this chapter, but now asks you to group them by subassembly rather than by hardware category.

Build Verification and Troubleshooting

The final skill this chapter teaches is knowing how to tell whether a build step, or the finished robot, actually works. Build verification means comparing your physical result against the manual's expected outcome, rather than assuming that "it looks about right" is good enough. On a robot, a part that looks correctly placed but is one shim off, or one screw loose, can still cause a wheel to bind or a chain to skip.

Verification should happen at two levels: after each individual step, as described earlier in the reading cycle, and again after each major phase (frame, drivetrain, battery mount, intake roller, wheels) is complete. At the end of the full 40-step build, spin every wheel by hand, check that the chain loops run smoothly without derailing, and confirm the battery sits securely in its mount.

When something does not work as expected, troubleshooting is the structured process of tracing a symptom back to its likely cause. The table below lists common issues builders encounter on a StarterBot chassis, paired with the most likely cause and fix, since recognizing these patterns quickly will save you from re-checking every single screw in the robot.

Symptom Likely Cause Fix
Wheel does not spin freely Bearing misaligned or shaft over-tightened by a collar Loosen the nearest collar, re-center the bearing, and re-tighten gradually
Chain slips or derails Sprockets not aligned on parallel shafts, or chain loop too loose Check that both sprockets sit at the same height, and shorten the chain loop by one link if needed
Screw will not tighten Wrong hex key size, or threads cross-threaded Confirm the hex key size matches the screw head; back the screw out fully and restart the threads
Locknut spins freely with no resistance Nylon insert worn out or wrong-size locknut used Replace with a new locknut of the correct thread size
Frame flexes or racks out of square A structural joint is missing a screw, or block mount is loose Recheck every screw at that joint against the manual's callout list
Battery shifts during handling Battery Strap not fully tightened Re-tighten the strap and confirm the Battery Mount is bolted to the frame, not just resting in place

!!! success If every wheel spins freely by hand, the chain runs without skipping, and the battery is locked into its mount, your StarterBot chassis has passed build verification and is ready for its first test drive.

??? note "Quick check: Why does a manual have you build subassemblies separately instead of building everything directly on the main frame? Click to reveal the answer." Because it is far easier to spot and fix a mistake in a small group of parts on the workbench, such as a shaft-bearing-hub unit, than to diagnose the same mistake after it is buried inside the fully assembled chassis. Subassemblies also let multiple people build different parts of the robot at the same time.

You now have a complete, verified StarterBot chassis and a working vocabulary for every category of part it contains: structural hardware, motion hardware, power and motor components, and fasteners. As the goBILDA manual puts it once the build is finished, "Now that the foundation is built, it's time to shift gears into programming, driver practice, and iterative design." The next chapters take that exact hardware you just assembled and explain the electrical, mechanical, and programming theory behind why it works, using the parts sitting in front of you as the running example.
