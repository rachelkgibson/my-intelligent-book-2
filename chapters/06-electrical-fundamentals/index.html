Electrical Fundamentals

Summary

Having built, programmed, and modified a robot, students now dig into why its electrical system works. This chapter covers core electrical theory: voltage, current, resistance, and Ohm's Law; the difference between direct and alternating current; series and parallel circuits; electrical power; and essential electrical safety practices, including how to recognize and prevent short circuits.

Stepping Back to Ask "Why"

Over the last five chapters, you built the StarterBot, learned the vocabulary of robotic systems, wrote programs to control it, and even designed custom parts for it. At every step, you trusted that flipping a switch would send power to the Yellow Jacket motors and the servos would spin exactly when your code told them to. This chapter steps back from building and asks a more basic question: what is actually happening, invisibly, inside the wires that connect your robot's 12V battery to its motors?

Electricity is the flow of electric charge through a material, and it is the invisible force that powers every motor, sensor, and controller on your robot. You cannot see electricity directly the way you can see a gear turn or a chain move, but you can measure its effects, predict its behavior with a small set of equations, and design circuits that use it safely. This chapter builds the theoretical foundation you will lean on for the rest of the course, starting with the three quantities that describe electricity in any circuit and ending with the safety habits that keep you and your robot protected.

Here is the arc this chapter will follow:

  • The three core quantities that describe electricity: voltage, current, and resistance
  • Ohm's Law, the single equation that ties those three quantities together
  • Direct current versus alternating current, and why your robot's battery supplies one and not the other
  • How circuits, series circuits, and parallel circuits organize the paths current can take
  • Electrical power, the rate at which a circuit does useful work
  • Conductors and insulators, the materials that either allow or block current
  • Electrical ground, short circuits, circuit protection, and the safety habits that tie it all together

The Water Analogy: Voltage, Current, and Resistance

Electrical quantities are difficult to picture because charge itself is invisible, so engineers often compare a circuit to a system of water flowing through pipes. Water needs pressure to push it through a pipe, it flows at some measurable rate, and the pipe itself offers some resistance to that flow depending on its width and length. Electric circuits behave the same way, with three matching quantities that you will use constantly for the rest of this course.

Voltage is the electrical pressure that pushes charge through a circuit, measured in volts (V). Just as water pressure pushes water through a pipe even when the pipe is not flowing, voltage exists across a battery's terminals whether or not a circuit is connected to it. The StarterBot's battery is rated at 12 volts, meaning it maintains 12 volts of electrical pressure across its terminals, ready to push current through the robot's motors the moment a switch closes the circuit.

Current is the rate at which electric charge flows past a point in a circuit, measured in amperes, or amps (A). In the water analogy, current is like the rate of water flow through the pipe, measured in gallons per minute. A Yellow Jacket motor spinning under load draws a measurable current from the battery, and a stalled motor (one blocked from turning) can draw a much larger current, which is one reason motor stalls are dangerous to a robot's electrical system.

Resistance is a material's opposition to the flow of electric current, measured in ohms (Ω, the Greek letter omega). A narrow pipe resists water flow more than a wide one, and in the same way, a long thin wire resists current flow more than a short thick one. Resistance is not always unwanted: some components, called resistors, are built specifically to add a controlled amount of resistance to a circuit, protecting sensitive parts from too much current.

Because these three quantities map so directly onto a water system, it helps to see them side by side before moving on to the equation that connects them.

Water System Electrical Circuit Unit
Water pressure from a pump Voltage Volts (V)
Rate of water flow through a pipe Current Amps (A)
Narrowness of the pipe Resistance Ohms (Ω)
Water flowing through the pipe Electric charge Coulombs (C)

!!! tip "The analogy has limits" Water is a physical substance that can pool or leak; electric charge in a simple circuit does not pool anywhere unless it is stored in a component like a capacitor or a battery. Use the water analogy to build intuition, but remember that current flows through a complete loop, not into empty space.

Ohm's Law: The Equation That Ties It Together

Voltage, current, and resistance are not independent of each other; they are locked together by one of the most useful equations in all of electronics. Ohm's Law states that the voltage across a circuit equals the current flowing through it multiplied by the resistance it encounters. Written as an equation, Ohm's Law is:

\[ V = IR \]

where \( V \) is voltage in volts, \( I \) is current in amps, and \( R \) is resistance in ohms. Because this equation relates three quantities, knowing any two lets you calculate the third. Rearranging the equation gives two more useful forms:

\[ I = \frac{V}{R} \]

\[ R = \frac{V}{I} \]

Consider the StarterBot's 12V battery connected to a simple resistive load with 4 ohms of resistance. Using the first rearranged equation, the current flowing through that load would be \( I = 12 \div 4 = 3 \) amps. If a different component in the same circuit had a much higher resistance of 60 ohms, only \( I = 12 \div 60 = 0.2 \) amps would flow. Higher resistance always means less current for the same voltage, which is exactly what the water-pipe analogy predicts: a narrower pipe lets less water through at the same pressure.

Before exploring the interactive tool below, notice that Ohm's Law describes a direct relationship between current and voltage, but an inverse relationship between current and resistance. The MicroSim lets you test both relationships by adjusting each value independently and watching the circuit respond.

Direct Current vs. Alternating Current

Ohm's Law works the same way regardless of which of two fundamentally different current types is flowing, but robots and household power rely on different ones. Direct current (DC) is electric current that flows in only one direction through a circuit, at a steady or slowly changing voltage. The StarterBot's 12V battery supplies direct current: electrons flow from the negative terminal, through the circuit, and back to the positive terminal, always in the same direction, which is exactly why the battery's voltage can be described with a single fixed number.

Alternating current (AC) is electric current that periodically reverses direction, cycling back and forth many times per second. The electricity that comes out of a wall outlet in the United States is AC, reversing direction 60 times per second. AC is efficient for transmitting power over long distances from power plants to homes, which is why household and industrial power grids use it, while battery-powered devices like the StarterBot almost always run on DC.

The two current types are suited to very different jobs, so it helps to compare them directly.

Feature Direct Current (DC) Alternating Current (AC)
Direction of flow One direction only Reverses periodically
Typical source Batteries, solar cells Power plants, wall outlets
StarterBot example 12V battery powering motors Not used on the robot itself
Common use Portable electronics, robots Long-distance power transmission

Because the StarterBot runs entirely on DC, every circuit concept for the rest of this chapter, and for the rest of this course, assumes current flowing steadily in one direction from the battery's positive terminal, through the robot's components, and back to its negative terminal.

Electrical Circuits: Series and Parallel Paths

Voltage alone cannot power a motor; the charge it pushes needs a complete path to travel before it can do any work. An electrical circuit is a closed loop that provides a continuous path for current to flow from a power source, through one or more components, and back to that source. If the loop is broken anywhere, by an open switch, a disconnected wire, or a burned-out component, current stops flowing entirely, the same way water stops flowing the instant a pipe is cut.

When a circuit contains more than one component, those components can be arranged in one of two basic patterns. A series circuit connects components one after another along a single path, so the same current flows through every component in turn, and the total voltage from the source is divided among them. A parallel circuit instead connects components across separate branches, so the full source voltage appears across each branch independently, while the total current from the source divides itself among the branches.

Imagine the StarterBot's two Yellow Jacket motors wired as loads on the 12V battery. If they were wired in series, current would have to pass through the first motor before reaching the second, meaning both motors would share the same current but split the battery's 12 volts between them, likely starving both motors of the voltage they need to run at full speed. Wiring them in parallel instead gives each motor the full 12 volts across its own branch, letting both motors run at full power while the battery supplies the combined current both motors draw. This is why robot drivetrains almost always wire their motors in parallel rather than in series.

Before comparing the two circuit types side by side in an interactive tool, notice the key trade-off: series circuits share current but divide voltage, while parallel circuits share voltage but divide current.

Electrical Power

Every circuit does work by converting electrical energy into some other form, whether that is motion in a motor, light in an LED, or heat in a resistor. Electrical power is the rate at which a circuit converts electrical energy, measured in watts (W). Power depends on both voltage and current, according to the equation:

\[ P = VI \]

where \( P \) is power in watts, \( V \) is voltage in volts, and \( I \) is current in amps. A Yellow Jacket motor drawing 2 amps from the StarterBot's 12V battery under normal driving load is converting \( P = 12 \times 2 = 24 \) watts of electrical power into mechanical motion and a small amount of waste heat.

Because power scales with both voltage and current, doubling either one doubles the power, and a motor stalled under load (drawing far more current than normal) can spike its power consumption dramatically, generating heat fast enough to damage the motor's windings if the current is not limited. Watching power in a circuit is one of the main reasons engineers care so much about current, since a fixed 12V battery means every increase in current is also an increase in power draw, and eventually in heat.

  • A slow-moving Yellow Jacket motor under light load might draw 0.5A, consuming 6W
  • A motor driving the robot at normal speed might draw 2A, consuming 24W
  • A stalled motor blocked from turning might spike to 10A or more, consuming over 120W as heat

Conductors and Insulators

Not every material lets current flow equally well, and the StarterBot's parts were chosen with this difference in mind. A conductor is a material that allows electric current to flow through it easily, because its atomic structure holds some electrons loosely enough to move freely when voltage is applied. Metals are the most common conductors: the StarterBot's steel 8mm REX shafts, its aluminum U-channels, and the copper wires running from the battery to the motors all conduct current readily, which is exactly why bare wire must never be allowed to touch the aluminum frame accidentally.

An insulator is a material that strongly resists the flow of electric current, holding its electrons tightly enough that they do not move freely even under voltage. Plastic, rubber, and most ceramics are common insulators. On the StarterBot, the plastic chain links, the rubber tread on the Gecko wheels, and the colored plastic jacket wrapped around every wire are all insulators, chosen specifically to keep current confined to the metal conductors inside them and away from a student's hands or the rest of the frame.

Recognizing conductors and insulators at a glance is a practical safety skill, not just a vocabulary exercise, so the interactive diagram below lets you test your own classification against real StarterBot parts before checking the answer.

Electrical Ground

Every circuit needs a shared reference point that all its voltages are measured against, and that point has a special name. Electrical ground is a common reference point in a circuit, typically connected to the negative terminal of the power source, against which all other voltages in the circuit are measured. On the StarterBot, the battery's negative terminal serves this role: every voltage measurement in the robot's electrical system, whether at a motor, a sensor, or a controller, is measured relative to that shared negative connection.

Ground serves two related purposes. First, it gives every component in the circuit a consistent zero-volt reference, so that a "12V signal" and a "5V signal" both mean something specific relative to the same baseline. Second, in many electrical systems, ground provides a deliberate low-resistance path that safely carries away stray or fault current, rather than letting it build up somewhere unpredictable, such as inside a control board or through a person's body.

Short Circuits and Circuit Protection

Grounding safely directs current along an intended path, but electrical faults can create unintended paths with almost no resistance at all. A short circuit occurs when current finds an unintended path of very low resistance, bypassing the intended load and allowing far more current to flow than the circuit was designed for. Recall from Ohm's Law that current equals voltage divided by resistance; when resistance drops close to zero, current spikes dramatically, generating heat fast enough to melt wire insulation, damage a battery, or start a fire.

A short circuit on the StarterBot might happen if a bare, uninsulated wire touched the aluminum frame in two places, creating an unintended low-resistance loop that skips the motor entirely. Because the frame conducts electricity well (it is aluminum, after all), this kind of accidental contact is exactly the failure mode that wire insulation and careful cable routing are designed to prevent.

Circuit protection refers to devices and design practices that limit current automatically to prevent damage from short circuits and other electrical faults. The most common circuit protection device is a fuse, a component containing a thin metal strip that melts and breaks the circuit if current exceeds a safe threshold, sacrificing itself to protect the rest of the system. Circuit breakers perform a similar job but can be reset after they trip, rather than being replaced.

Protection Device How It Works Reusable?
Fuse Thin metal strip melts and breaks the circuit above a set current No, must be replaced
Circuit breaker Mechanical switch trips open above a set current Yes, can be reset
Polyfuse (resettable) Resistance rises sharply when overheated, self-resets when cooled Yes, resets automatically

Electrical Safety

Everything covered in this chapter, from Ohm's Law to circuit protection, comes together in a handful of practical safety habits every robotics student should follow around the StarterBot's electrical system. Electrical safety is the set of practices and precautions that prevent electrical shock, fire, and equipment damage while working with circuits.

  • Disconnect the battery before working on any wiring, motor connection, or exposed terminal
  • Inspect wire insulation regularly for cuts, fraying, or exposed conductor, especially near moving parts like sprockets and chain
  • Never let bare wire touch the aluminum frame, since the frame is a conductor and can create an unintended short circuit
  • Keep the battery's connections secure so vibration during driving cannot loosen a terminal and create arcing
  • Respect fuses and circuit breakers as protection, not inconvenience; never bypass one with a piece of wire

!!! danger "Never bypass circuit protection" A fuse or breaker that trips repeatedly is telling you something is wrong somewhere in the circuit, likely a short circuit or an overloaded motor. Replacing a fuse with a higher-rated one, or with a plain wire, removes the one safeguard designed to stop a fire before it starts.

With voltage, current, resistance, Ohm's Law, circuit types, power, materials, grounding, and protection all in place, you now have the theoretical toolkit to understand exactly what is happening every time the StarterBot's battery powers a motor. The next chapter builds directly on this foundation, moving from electrical theory into the specific electronic components, like resistors, capacitors, and diodes, that make up real circuit designs.

??? note "Quick check: Why do the StarterBot's two drive motors share the battery's 12 volts equally instead of splitting it? - Click to expand" Because they are wired in parallel, not in series. A parallel circuit gives each branch the full source voltage, while only the total current supplied by the battery divides among the branches, letting both motors run at full power simultaneously.
