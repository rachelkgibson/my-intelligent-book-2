Designing and Building Custom Parts

Summary

With a working, programmable robot in hand, students now learn how to improve and extend it. This chapter covers the full engineering design process: defining problems, setting design constraints and criteria, brainstorming, sketching, prototyping, iterating, testing, and documenting a design, including trade-off analysis and reverse engineering. Students then apply this process to manufacture custom parts for their robot using additive manufacturing and 3D printing, and practice team-based design and engineering notebook documentation along the way.

The Engineering Design Process

Every custom part on a competition robot starts as a problem, not a drawing. The engineering design process is a repeatable, structured cycle that engineers use to move from a problem statement to a working solution, then loop back around as they learn more. Unlike a single flash of inspiration, the process is deliberately iterative: it expects you to revisit earlier steps as new information appears, instead of marching through a fixed sequence one time and declaring victory.

Picture a real problem that will run through this entire chapter. Your FTC team has been driving the goBILDA StarterBot you assembled in Chapter 1 and programmed in Chapter 4, and two things keep going wrong during practice matches. Game pieces bounce off the front of the intake instead of feeding into the rollers, and the distance sensor your team zip-tied near the intake keeps twisting out of alignment after every match. Nothing in the stock StarterBot kit solves either problem, so your team decides to design and 3D print a custom part: a combined intake guide and sensor mount you will call the Guide-Mount for the rest of this chapter.

The engineering design process typically includes the following stages, though real teams move back and forth between them rather than only moving forward:

  1. Define the problem
  2. Establish design constraints and criteria
  3. Brainstorm possible solutions
  4. Create concept sketches
  5. Build a prototype
  6. Test the prototype
  7. Iterate on the design
  8. Document the final design and specifications

Notice that testing and iteration point back toward brainstorming and sketching rather than toward some final finish line. A prototype that fails a test does not mean starting over from nothing; it means the team learned something and cycles through the relevant earlier steps again with better information. Problem Definition

The first stage of the process, problem definition, means writing a clear, specific statement of exactly what needs to be solved before anyone sketches a single idea. A vague complaint like "the intake doesn't work very well" is not a problem definition; it describes a symptom, not the actual problem an engineer can design against. A strong problem definition names the specific failure, the conditions under which it happens, and why it matters.

Your team's problem definition for this chapter might read: "During competition matches, roughly one in three game pieces that touch the front bumper bounce away instead of entering the intake rollers, and the zip-tied distance sensor loses its aim after contact, causing missed detections." Notice how specific this is compared to "the intake needs work." A precise problem definition keeps the whole team focused on the same target and prevents wasted effort brainstorming solutions to the wrong problem.

Good problem definitions generally answer four questions:

  • What exactly is going wrong, in observable, measurable terms?
  • Under what conditions does it happen (every match, only at certain angles, only after several cycles)?
  • Who or what is affected by the problem?
  • Why does solving it matter to the team's goals?

Design Constraints and Design Criteria

Once a team agrees on the problem, it needs rules for judging any solution before brainstorming begins. Design constraints are fixed limits that any acceptable solution absolutely must satisfy; a design that violates a constraint is disqualified, no matter how clever it is otherwise. Design criteria, by contrast, are the qualities used to judge and rank solutions that already meet every constraint, so a team can decide which acceptable design is actually the best one.

For the Guide-Mount, a constraint might be "must bolt to existing StarterBot channel holes without drilling new holes in the chassis," because FTC competition rules and the team's limited tools make redrilling impractical, not just undesirable. A criterion, on the other hand, might be "should be as lightweight as possible," because a lighter bracket is preferred, but a slightly heavier one that still fits and functions is not automatically disqualified.

The table below reinforces this distinction with concrete Guide-Mount examples, so the difference between an absolute limit and a preference is grounded in the running project rather than left abstract.

Category Question it answers Guide-Mount example
Design constraint What MUST the design satisfy to be acceptable at all? Must mount using existing goBILDA U-channel hole spacing
Design constraint What MUST the design satisfy to be acceptable at all? Must not block the intake roller's rotation
Design criterion What makes one acceptable design BETTER than another? Should minimize total mass added to the robot
Design criterion What makes one acceptable design BETTER than another? Should be quick to remove for maintenance

Brainstorming and Concept Sketching

With constraints and criteria in hand, the team moves to brainstorming, a divergent-thinking activity where the goal is generating as many possible solution ideas as possible without judging or eliminating any of them yet. Brainstorming works best when teams defer criticism until later; judging ideas too early tends to shut down the unusual suggestions that sometimes turn out to be the best ones.

Effective brainstorming sessions generally follow a few shared rules:

  • Defer judgment: no idea gets criticized during the session itself
  • Go for quantity: more ideas increase the odds that a strong one appears
  • Build on others' ideas rather than only proposing your own
  • Encourage wild ideas, since a rough idea can often be refined later

For the Guide-Mount, a fifteen-minute brainstorm might produce ideas ranging from a simple angled ramp, to a curved funnel wall, to a two-piece clamp that grips the existing channel and cradles the sensor in a rigid pocket. Once the list is long enough, the team moves to concept sketching: quickly drawing rough, low-detail pictures of the most promising ideas so they can be compared side by side. A concept sketch is not a technical drawing; it is a fast, approximate picture meant to communicate the basic shape and function of an idea in a minute or two, not to specify exact dimensions.

Prototyping

A concept sketch shows what an idea might look like, but it cannot tell you whether the idea actually works on the real robot. Prototyping closes that gap by building a physical, working (or partially working) model of a design so the team can test it quickly and cheaply before committing to a final version. Prototypes exist on a spectrum from rough to refined, and skipping straight to a refined prototype often wastes time on ideas that fail for simple, avoidable reasons.

Teams typically move through prototype fidelity in stages:

  1. Low-fidelity prototype: cardboard, tape, and hot glue mocked up in minutes, testing rough shape and fit only
  2. Medium-fidelity prototype: a 3D-printed part in a fast, low-detail print setting, testing real fit and basic function
  3. High-fidelity prototype: a carefully printed, dimensionally accurate part, close to or identical to the final design

For the Guide-Mount, the team's first prototype is a piece of cardboard taped to the chassis in the shape of the angled ramp idea, just to confirm the geometry does not collide with the intake rollers before spending any filament. Only after the cardboard mock-up survives a few test cycles does the team move on to an actual 3D-printed version.

Design Iteration and Design Testing

Design testing means evaluating a prototype against the constraints and criteria established earlier, using a real robot and real conditions rather than guesswork. For the Guide-Mount, testing means mounting each printed prototype on the StarterBot and running repeated intake cycles with practice game pieces, recording how many pieces feed cleanly into the rollers and whether the sensor holds its aim after contact.

Design iteration is what happens next: using the test results to revise the design, then testing the revised version again. Iteration is not a sign that the first attempt failed completely; it is the expected, normal way a design improves. The table below shows how the Guide-Mount actually improved across two rounds of testing and iteration.

Test round Result Iteration made
Round 1 (angled ramp) 6 of 10 pieces fed cleanly; sensor still shifted slightly on hard contact Added a rigid pocket around the sensor and steepened the ramp angle
Round 2 (ramp + sensor pocket) 9 of 10 pieces fed cleanly; sensor held aim through all 10 cycles Design accepted; minor edge rounded to reduce piece bounce further

Iteration can loop a design through testing several times before it is considered finished, and a team should budget time for at least one full iteration cycle rather than assuming the first printed version will be the last.

Trade-Off Analysis and Reverse Engineering

Sometimes two candidate designs each satisfy every constraint, yet one scores higher on some criteria and lower on others; a thinner bracket might be lighter but less durable, while a thicker one might be stronger but heavier and slower to print. Trade-off analysis is the structured comparison engineers use to decide between such options by scoring each candidate against weighted criteria, rather than choosing by gut feeling alone.

A simple trade-off analysis assigns each criterion a weight based on importance, scores each design option, and multiplies weight by score to get a total. The weighted decision matrix below compares three Guide-Mount shapes the team actually considered, using a 1-5 scale where higher is better.

Criterion (weight) Angled ramp Curved funnel Two-piece clamp
Print time (x2) 5 (fast) 3 (medium) 2 (slow)
Feed reliability (x3) 4 5 3
Ease of removal (x1) 4 3 5
Weighted total 34 34 24

Notice that the angled ramp and curved funnel tie overall, even though they score differently on individual criteria; a trade-off analysis often narrows a field to a couple of strong finalists rather than a single obvious winner, leaving room for the team to break the tie using practical factors like available print time before competition.

Before finalizing a shape, many teams also practice reverse engineering: studying an existing part or design closely to understand how and why it works, in order to inform a new design. Your team reverse engineers the stock goBILDA U-channel by measuring its hole spacing and pattern rather than guessing, so the Guide-Mount's mounting tabs align perfectly with existing holes instead of requiring new ones drilled into the chassis. Reverse engineering an existing, proven part is often faster and more reliable than designing mounting geometry from scratch.

Design Specifications and Bill of Materials

Once trade-off analysis and reverse engineering point to a final shape, that shape needs to become a precise, buildable description. Design specifications are the exact, measurable technical details a finished design must meet, translated directly from the team's constraints and criteria into numbers: overall dimensions, hole spacing, wall thickness, maximum mass, and required material.

A specification for the Guide-Mount might state: overall length 65mm, mounting tab spacing matching the standard goBILDA hole pitch, minimum wall thickness 2mm for strength, maximum mass 12 grams, and clearance of at least 3mm around the intake roller shaft. Specifications like these give both the CAD designer and the 3D printer operator an unambiguous target, rather than leaving details open to interpretation late in the project.

Alongside specifications, a finished design also needs a Bill of Materials, the complete list of every item required to actually produce the part, including quantities. A 3D-printed part's BOM looks different from the kit BOM you read in Chapter 1, since it must account for filament and print time rather than pre-made components.

Item Quantity Notes
PLA filament 18 grams Estimated from sliced model
M3 heat-set insert 2 For sensor mounting screws
M3 x 8mm screw 2 Secures distance sensor to bracket
Print time 55 minutes At chosen layer height and infill

Design Documentation and the Engineering Notebook

Every decision made across problem definition, brainstorming, trade-off analysis, and testing is only useful to the team later if someone wrote it down. Design documentation is the complete record of a project: sketches, test data, specifications, the Bill of Materials, and the reasoning behind key decisions, kept together so the process can be reviewed, defended, or repeated. Many FTC competitions specifically judge teams on design documentation as part of an Engineering Design or Innovate award.

The primary tool for design documentation is the engineering notebook, a dated, chronological journal, physical or digital, where a team records its work as it happens rather than reconstructing it from memory afterward. A strong engineering notebook entry for a single work session typically includes:

  • The date and names of team members present
  • The problem or task worked on that session
  • Sketches, photos, or screenshots created during the session
  • Data collected, such as test results or measurements
  • Decisions made and the reasoning behind them

!!! tip Record failed prototypes and rejected ideas in the notebook too, not only successes. Judges reviewing an FTC engineering notebook are often more interested in how a team responded to a failed test than in a design that worked perfectly on the first try.

Team-Based Design

None of the stages above happen in isolation on a real FTC team; they happen through team-based design, where multiple students share responsibility across roles, communicate constantly, and reach decisions together rather than leaving every choice to a single member. Brainstorming benefits from a range of perspectives, trade-off analysis benefits from multiple people scoring options independently before comparing notes, and documentation benefits from someone specifically responsible for keeping the notebook current.

A team working on the Guide-Mount might divide responsibilities roughly as follows:

  • A mechanical lead who owns the CAD model and print settings
  • A testing lead who runs intake cycles and records results
  • A notebook keeper who documents each session's decisions and data
  • A build lead who installs prototypes on the robot and checks for interference

Dividing roles does not mean working separately; it means each stage of the engineering design process has a clear owner making sure that step actually gets done, while decisions like which shape to prototype next are still made as a group. Communication matters as much as any single role: a mechanical lead who changes a dimension without telling the testing lead can waste an entire test session on an outdated prototype, and a notebook keeper who is not included in trade-off discussions cannot document the reasoning behind them accurately.

Manufacturing Process

Once the Guide-Mount's specifications are locked in, the team faces one final question: how do you actually turn a digital design into a physical part? A manufacturing process is the sequence of steps that transforms raw material into a finished physical object matching a design's specifications. Manufacturing processes fall into two broad families, and understanding both helps explain why FTC teams so often choose the one covered in the next section.

Subtractive manufacturing starts with a solid block of material and removes material, using tools like drills, mills, or saws, until only the desired shape remains, the same way a sculptor carves a statue from a block of stone. Additive manufacturing, covered in detail next, instead builds an object up from nothing, adding material only where the design calls for it. Neither family is universally better: subtractive processes like CNC milling can produce very strong metal parts but struggle with hollow internal shapes and waste the material that gets cut away, while additive processes handle complex internal geometry easily and waste very little material, but usually in weaker plastics rather than machined metal. Additive Manufacturing and 3D Printing

Additive manufacturing is the broad family of manufacturing processes that build a physical object by adding material layer by layer from a digital 3D model, rather than removing it from a larger block. Several distinct technologies fall under this umbrella. Stereolithography (SLA) cures liquid resin with a laser, and Selective Laser Sintering (SLS) fuses powdered material layer by layer, both common in industrial and professional settings. The version most accessible to FTC teams, schools, and hobbyists is 3D printing, specifically a process called Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), which melts a plastic filament and extrudes it through a heated nozzle, tracing each layer before moving up to print the next one directly on top of it.

Turning the Guide-Mount's finished CAD model into a physical part requires slicing software, a program that converts the 3D model into a set of printer instructions (G-code) and lets the team choose print settings that affect the final part's strength, weight, and print time. Two of the most important settings are layer height, the thickness of each printed layer, and infill percentage, how solid or hollow the interior of the part is filled with a repeating support pattern. Thinner layers and higher infill generally produce stronger, heavier, slower-to-print parts, while thicker layers and lower infill print faster and lighter but weaker. Explore how these two settings trade off against each other in the interactive tool below before your own team chooses settings for its first Guide-Mount print.

With print settings chosen, the team slices the final Guide-Mount model, sends the resulting file to the school's 3D printer, and roughly an hour later removes a finished bracket that bolts directly onto the StarterBot's existing channel holes. The distance sensor sits in its rigid pocket instead of a zip tie, and the ramp guides game pieces into the intake rollers instead of letting them bounce away. The part exists because the team walked the entire engineering design process end to end: a defined problem, clear constraints and criteria, a wide brainstorm narrowed by sketching, a cardboard prototype that became a printed one, two rounds of testing and iteration, a trade-off analysis that settled the final shape, and a notebook documenting every step along the way. The same cycle you just walked through for one bracket is exactly what real FTC teams repeat, part after part, all season long.
